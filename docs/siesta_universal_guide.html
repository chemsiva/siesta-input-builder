Foundations of SIESTA & First-Principles Physics

The Quantum Many-Body Challenge and Hohenberg-Kohn Theorems

In condensed matter physics and quantum chemistry, the fundamental goal is to solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation for a collection of NN interacting electrons and MM atomic nuclei: ĤΨ(𝐫1,…,𝐫N;𝐑1,…,𝐑M)=EΨ(𝐫1,…,𝐫N;𝐑1,…,𝐑M)\hat{H} \Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N; \mathbf{R}_1, \dots, \mathbf{R}_M) = E \Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N; \mathbf{R}_1, \dots, \mathbf{R}_M) Under the Born-Oppenheimer approximation, because atomic nuclei are thousands of times more massive than electrons (Mnuc≫meM_{\text{nuc}} \gg m_e), nuclear kinetic energy is decoupled, treating nuclei as static external point charges that produce a Coulomb potential Vext(𝐫)V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) felt by the electrons.

Even with this simplification, the exact electronic wave function Ψ(𝐫1,…,𝐫N)\Psi(\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_N) depends on 3N3N spatial coordinates. For a modest semiconductor slab containing 100 atoms (∼1000\sim 1000 valence electrons), Ψ\Psi lives in a 3000-dimensional Hilbert space, rendering direct wave function approaches computationally impossible for solid-state materials.

Density Functional Theory (DFT) circumvents this exponential bottleneck through the two foundational theorems of Hohenberg and Kohn (1964):

  1. Theorem 1 (One-to-One Mapping): The ground-state electron density ρ(𝐫)\rho(\mathbf{r}) uniquely determines the external potential Vext(𝐫)V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) up to an additive constant, and therefore uniquely determines the full Hamiltonian and all ground-state observables of the many-body system.

  2. Theorem 2 (Variational Principle): There exists a universal energy functional E[ρ]E[\rho] such that the exact ground-state density minimizes E[ρ]E[\rho], yielding the true ground-state energy E0E_0: E0≤E[ρ̃]for any trial density ρ̃(𝐫)≥0,∫ρ̃(𝐫)d3𝐫=NE_0 \le E[\tilde{\rho}] \quad \text{for any trial density } \tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{r}) \ge 0, \quad \int \tilde{\rho}(\mathbf{r}) d^3\mathbf{r} = N

The Kohn-Sham Formulation

To make this variational principle computationally tractable, Kohn and Sham (1965) replaced the intractable interacting electron system with an auxiliary, non-interacting electron system that yields the exact same ground-state density ρ(𝐫)\rho(\mathbf{r}). The total energy functional is partitioned into four physically transparent terms: E[ρ]=Ts[ρ]+∫ρ(𝐫)Vext(𝐫)d3𝐫+EH[ρ]+Exc[ρ]E[\rho] = T_s[\rho] + \int \rho(\mathbf{r}) V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) d^3\mathbf{r} + E_H[\rho] + E_{\text{xc}}[\rho] where:

  • Ts[ρ]=−ℏ22m∑i=1N∫ψi*(𝐫)∇2ψi(𝐫)d3𝐫T_s[\rho] = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \sum_{i=1}^N \int \psi_i^*(\mathbf{r}) \nabla^2 \psi_i(\mathbf{r}) d^3\mathbf{r} is the kinetic energy of non-interacting electrons described by single-particle Kohn-Sham orbitals ψi(𝐫)\psi_i(\mathbf{r}).

  • EH[ρ]=e22∬ρ(𝐫)ρ(𝐫′)|𝐫−𝐫′|d3𝐫d3𝐫′E_H[\rho] = \frac{e^2}{2} \iint \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r})\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|} d^3\mathbf{r} d^3\mathbf{r}' is the classical electrostatic Hartree electron-electron repulsion.

  • Exc[ρ]E_{\text{xc}}[\rho] is the Exchange-Correlation (XC) functional, which encapsulates all quantum mechanical many-body effects: Pauli exchange repulsion and dynamic electron correlation.

Varying E[ρ]E[\rho] with respect to ψi*(𝐫)\psi_i^*(\mathbf{r}) yields the canonical Kohn-Sham Equations: (−ℏ22m∇2+Veff[ρ](𝐫))ψi(𝐫)=εiψi(𝐫),where ρ(𝐫)=∑i=1N|ψi(𝐫)|2\left( -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V_{\text{eff}}[\rho](\mathbf{r}) \right) \psi_i(\mathbf{r}) = \varepsilon_i \psi_i(\mathbf{r}), \quad \text{where } \rho(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{i=1}^N |\psi_i(\mathbf{r})|^2 with the effective single-particle potential defined as: Veff(𝐫)=Vext(𝐫)+e2∫ρ(𝐫′)|𝐫−𝐫′|d3𝐫′+δExc[ρ]δρ(𝐫)V_{\text{eff}}(\mathbf{r}) = V_{\text{ext}}(\mathbf{r}) + e^2 \int \frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|} d^3\mathbf{r}' + \frac{\delta E_{\text{xc}}[\rho]}{\delta \rho(\mathbf{r})} Because VeffV_{\text{eff}} depends on ρ(𝐫)\rho(\mathbf{r}), which in turn depends on the unknown wavefunctions ψi(𝐫)\psi_i(\mathbf{r}), the Kohn-Sham equations must be solved iteratively until self-consistency is achieved (Self-Consistent Field, SCF).

Why SIESTA? The O(N)O(N) Localized Orbital Paradigm

SIESTA (Spanish Initiative for Electronic Simulations with Thousands of Atoms) was conceived by Emilio Artacho, Alberto García, Julian Gale, Javier Junquera, Richard Martin, Pablo Ordejón, Daniel Sánchez-Portal, and José M. Soler.

The defining breakthrough of SIESTA is that its basis orbitals ϕμ(𝐫)\phi_{\mu}(\mathbf{r}) are strictly zero beyond a finite cutoff radius rc,μr_{c,\mu}: ϕμ(𝐫)≡0for |𝐫−𝐑I|>rc,μ\phi_{\mu}(\mathbf{r}) \equiv 0 \quad \text{for } |\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{R}_I| > r_{c,\mu} Because orbitals strictly vanish beyond rcr_c, the overlap matrix Sμν=⟨ϕμ|ϕν⟩S_{\mu\nu} = \langle \phi_{\mu} | \phi_{\nu} \rangle and Hamiltonian matrix Hμν=⟨ϕμ|Ĥ|ϕν⟩H_{\mu\nu} = \langle \phi_{\mu} | \hat{H} | \phi_{\nu} \rangle are sparse banded matrices: matrix elements are strictly zero unless two atoms are within distance rc,μ+rc,νr_{c,\mu} + r_{c,\nu}. As a direct consequence:

  • Matrix construction scales strictly as 𝒪(N)\mathcal{O}(N).

  • Storing matrices requires memory proportional to 𝒪(N)\mathcal{O}(N) rather than 𝒪(N2)\mathcal{O}(N^2).

  • Systems comprising thousands of atoms (e.g. FAPbI3FAPbI_3 perovskite slabs with organic ligands) can be calculated on standard computing clusters where plane-wave codes would run out of memory.

The FDF (Flexible Data Format) Language

Syntax, Tokens, and Data Blocks

All control parameters, structural descriptions, and calculation directives in SIESTA are provided through a unified plain-text file known as the FDF (Flexible Data Format) file (conventionally named with the .fdf extension).

Key rules of the FDF format:

  1. Case Insensitivity: FDF keywords are case-insensitive. MeshCutoff, meshcutoff, and Mesh.Cutoff resolve to the exact same parameter.

  2. Punctuation Tolerance: Characters like periods (.), hyphens (-), and underscores (_) in variable names are largely treated as interchangeable separators in modern SIESTA versions.

  3. Single Values: Specified as Keyword Value [Unit]:

    MeshCutoff         350.0 Ry
ElectronicTemperature  25.0 meV

  4. Logical Values: Booleans can be specified using true/false, yes/no, or single letters T/F.

  5. Data Blocks: Structured arrays, coordinate tables, and matrix specifications are enclosed in %block BlockName and %endblock BlockName:

    %block ChemicalSpeciesLabel
  1   14  Si      Si.psf
%endblock ChemicalSpeciesLabel

  6. Comments: Any text preceded by # or ! is treated as a comment and ignored by the parser.

Surface Slabs, Vacuum Padding, and Dipole Correction

When modeling surfaces, interfaces, or 2D materials (graphene, perovskite nanosheets, transition metal dichalcogenides), three-dimensional periodic boundary conditions (PBC) are still enforced by the FFT grid. To prevent spurious electronic interactions between periodic vertical images of the slab along the surface normal (zz), one must:

  1. Add Sufficient Vacuum: Typically 15 Å≤dvac≤25 Å15\text{ \AA} \le d_{\text{vac}} \le 25\text{ \AA} between the highest atom of one slab and the lowest atom of the periodic replica.

  2. Center the Slab: Shift atomic coordinates so the vacuum is evenly split above and below the material (AtomicCoordinatesOrigin COP-Z).

  3. Apply Dipole Correction: If the slab is asymmetric (e.g., polar surface termination or molecule adsorbed on one side only), an artificial net dipole moment 𝐩\mathbf{p} arises across the cell. This induces a fictitious linear electric field across the vacuum. Setting:

    Slab.DipoleCorrection true

    instructs SIESTA to introduce an internal compensating dipole sheet in the vacuum gap, flattening the potential and yielding exact, uncorrupted work functions.

Pseudopotentials & PAO Basis Sets

The Pseudopotential Concept (Norm-Conservation & Kleinman-Bylander)

Core electrons (e.g. 1s22s22p61s^2 2s^2 2p^6 in Silicon) are tightly bound to the nucleus and chemically inert. However, their wavefunctions oscillate violently near the core to maintain orthogonality with deeper shells. In pseudopotential theory:

  1. Core electrons and the nuclear Coulomb potential are replaced by an effective smooth pseudopotential Vpseudo(r)V_{\text{pseudo}}(r).

  2. Beyond a core radius rcr_c, the pseudo-wavefunction ψ̃(r)\tilde{\psi}(r) matches the all-electron wavefunction ψ(r)\psi(r) exactly.

  3. Under norm-conservation, the integral of |ψ̃(r)|2|\tilde{\psi}(r)|^2 inside rcr_c equals that of the all-electron wavefunction, ensuring correct electrostatic scattering properties across varying chemical environments.

SIESTA requires norm-conserving pseudopotentials in the separable Kleinman-Bylander (KB) form: V̂NL=∑l,m|χlm⟩⟨χlm|⟨χlm|δVl|χlm⟩\hat{V}_{\text{NL}} = \sum_{l,m} \frac{| \chi_{lm} \rangle \langle \chi_{lm} |}{\langle \chi_{lm} | \delta V_l | \chi_{lm} \rangle}

Pseudopotential File Formats: PSF and Modern PSML

  • PSF (Traditional ASCII Format): Stores radial grid data, logarithmic derivatives, and local/non-local projectors. Named [Element].psf (e.g., Pb.psf, I.psf).

  • PSML (Pseudopotential Markup Language): The modern XML-based standard adopted by SIESTA 5.x. Enables full provenance tracking, relativistic spin-orbit projectors, and semicore configuration verification.

Numerical Atomic Orbitals (NAOs) and Basis Cardinality

Unlike Gaussian orbitals used in quantum chemistry, SIESTA computes radial basis functions numerically by solving the Schrödinger equation for the isolated pseudo-atom enclosed in a spherical confinement well: ϕnlm(𝐫)=Rnl(r)Ylm(𝐫̂)\phi_{nlm}(\mathbf{r}) = R_{nl}(r) Y_{lm}(\hat{\mathbf{r}}) The basis set hierarchy is defined by PAO.BasisSize:

  • SZ (Single-Zeta): Minimal basis. Exactly one radial function per valence orbital (e.g. for Carbon 2s,2p2s, 2p: 1 ss function and 3 pp functions = 4 basis functions). Fast, qualitative.

  • DZ (Double-Zeta): Two radial functions per valence orbital (2s,2s′,2p,2p′2s, 2s', 2p, 2p'). Essential for chemical bonding and valence deformation.

  • DZP (Double-Zeta Polarized): The standard for production research. Takes DZ and adds a polarization shell with angular momentum l+1l+1 (e.g. adds 3d3d orbitals to Carbon 2p2p). Allows electron clouds to distort under asymmetric surface fields.

  • TZP / TZ2P: Triple-zeta with multiple polarization shells. High-precision limit, approaching the complete basis set (CBS) plane-wave accuracy.

The Spatial Confinement Parameter: PAO.EnergyShift

How does SIESTA determine where to truncate the orbital so it strictly equals zero at rcr_c? Instead of forcing the user to guess radii for every shell of every element, SIESTA provides the universal thermodynamic parameter PAO.EnergyShift: δE=ℏ2π22merc,nl2\delta E = \frac{\hbar^2 \pi^2}{2 m_e r_{c,nl}^2}

  • Physical Meaning: It represents the energy increase experienced by an electron when it is confined inside a spherical box of radius rcr_c.

  • Standard Value: Default is 100 meV to 250 meV.

  • Behavior: A smaller energy shift (e.g. 50 meV) gives a larger rcr_c, expanding the basis set to capture diffuse vacuum states at the cost of denser matrices. A larger energy shift (e.g. 300 meV) makes orbitals more compact and faster, but may under-represent surface dipole relaxation.

Real-Space Grid & Exchange-Correlation (XC)

The Real-Space Mesh and MeshCutoff

While Hamiltonian and overlap matrices are computed on atomic basis orbitals, the electron density ρ(𝐫)\rho(\mathbf{r}) and Hartree/XC potentials are represented on a 3D regular Cartesian real-space grid. The grid fineness is specified by MeshCutoff: Ecut=ℏ2kmax22me⟹Δx≈πkmax=π2meEcutE_{\text{cut}} = \frac{\hbar^2 k_{\text{max}}^2}{2 m_e} \implies \Delta x \approx \frac{\pi}{k_{\text{max}}} = \frac{\pi}{\sqrt{2 m_e E_{\text{cut}}}}

  • For Ecut=200 Ry⟹Δx≈0.12 ÅE_{\text{cut}} = 200\text{ Ry} \implies \Delta x \approx 0.12\text{ \AA}.

  • For Ecut=350 Ry⟹Δx≈0.09 ÅE_{\text{cut}} = 350\text{ Ry} \implies \Delta x \approx 0.09\text{ \AA}.

  • Rule of Thumb: Always perform a mesh cutoff convergence test. Standard semiconductors require 250–350 Ry250\text{--}350\text{ Ry}. Highly localized elements (O,F,FeO, F, Fe) require 350–450 Ry350\text{--}450\text{ Ry}.

The Eggbox Effect

Because the real-space grid is discrete and does not automatically displace with the atoms as they move, shifting an atom across grid points induces an artificial periodic ripple in the total energy and forces, termed the eggbox effect:

  • Remedy 1: Increase MeshCutoff (>300 Ry> 300\text{ Ry}).

  • Remedy 2: Use modern grid-cell sampling (GridCellSampling true).

  • Remedy 3: Use soft confinement potentials (PAO.SoftDefault true) to smooth the orbital second derivatives near rcr_c.

Exchange-Correlation (XC) Functionals

SIESTA supports both local and gradient-corrected approximations:

XC.functional   GGA

  • GGA (Generalized Gradient Approximation): XC.functional GGA, XC.authors PBE (Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof). The workhorse functional for solid-state chemistry and surfaces.

  • PBEsol: XC.authors PBEsol. Refined GGA specifically tuned for equilibrium lattice constants and bulk phonon frequencies.

  • van der Waals Dispersion (Grimme DFT-D3): Crucial for molecular adsorption, perovskite organic-inorganic interfaces, and layered materials:

    DFTD3           true

  1. Collinear Spin Polarized: α\alpha and β\beta spins along a single quantization axis:

    Spin            polarized

    Spin            spin-orbit

Self-Consistent Field (SCF) Control

The Density Matrix Formulation

Instead of dealing with individual Kohn-Sham wavefunctions, SIESTA expresses all physical observables via the single-particle density matrix PμνP_{\mu\nu}: Pμν=∑ificμicνi*P_{\mu\nu} = \sum_i f_i c_{\mu i} c_{\nu i}^* where fif_i is the Fermi-Dirac occupation factor. The real-space electron density is reconstructed as: ρ(𝐫)=∑μ,νPμνϕμ*(𝐫)ϕν(𝐫)\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \sum_{\mu,\nu} P_{\mu\nu} \phi_{\mu}^*(\mathbf{r}) \phi_{\nu}(\mathbf{r})

SCF Convergence Criteria and Mixing Algorithms

During each SCF iteration mm, the computed density matrix P(m)P^{(m)} differs from the input density matrix Pin(m)P_{\text{in}}^{(m)}. A simple substitution Pin(m+1)=P(m)P_{\text{in}}^{(m+1)} = P^{(m)} leads to violent charge sloshing and divergence. Therefore, mixing algorithms are applied:

MaxSCFIterations       150
DM.Tolerance           1.0d-4
DM.MixingWeight        0.25
DM.NumberPulay         8

  • DM.Tolerance: The calculation is declared converged when: maxμ,ν|Pμν(m)−Pμν(m−1)|<𝙳𝙼.𝚃𝚘𝚕𝚎𝚛𝚊𝚗𝚌𝚎(typically 10−4 to 10−5)\max_{\mu,\nu} |P_{\mu\nu}^{(m)} - P_{\mu\nu}^{(m-1)}| < \texttt{DM.Tolerance} \quad (\text{typically } 10^{-4}\text{ to } 10^{-5})

  • DM.MixingWeight (α\alpha): Fraction of the newly calculated density matrix mixed with the old matrix. For difficult systems (surfaces, transition metals, low bandgap perovskites), lower this value to 0.05 or 0.10 to prevent divergence.

  • DM.NumberPulay: Depth of the Pulay Direct Inversion in the Iterative Subspace (DIIS) history. Using 6 to 8 history steps accelerates convergence exponentially after the initial linear regime.

Electronic Temperature (Fermi Smearing)

For metallic systems or surfaces with near-zero bandgaps, sharp discontinuities in Fermi occupations at T=0T=0 destabilize SCF convergence. Introducing a finite electronic temperature smears occupations via the Fermi-Dirac function: f(ε)=1e(ε−EF)/kBTelec+1f(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon - E_F)/k_B T_{\text{elec}}} + 1}

ElectronicTemperature  25.0 meV

This represents T≈290 KT \approx 290\text{ K}, smoothing occupation transitions and ensuring rapid, monotonic convergence.

Brillouin Zone Sampling & K-Points

Reciprocal Space Integration & Monkhorst-Pack Grids

In crystalline solids, translational symmetry dictates that wavefunctions obey Bloch’s theorem: ψn𝐤(𝐫)=ei𝐤⋅𝐫un𝐤(𝐫)\psi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}) = e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}} u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}). Physical properties require integration over the first Brillouin zone: g‾=Ω(2π)3∫BZg(𝐤)d3𝐤≈∑𝐤w𝐤g(𝐤)\bar{g} = \frac{\Omega}{(2\pi)^3} \int_{\text{BZ}} g(\mathbf{k}) d^3\mathbf{k} \approx \sum_{\mathbf{k}} w_{\mathbf{k}} g(\mathbf{k}) SIESTA generates discrete kk-point grids using the Monkhorst-Pack (MP) scheme:

%block kgrid_Monkhorst_Pack
  4   0   0   0.5
  0   4   0   0.5
  0   0   1   0.0
%endblock kgrid_Monkhorst_Pack

The first three numbers in each row define the divisions along reciprocal vectors 𝐛1,𝐛2,𝐛3\mathbf{b}_1, \mathbf{b}_2, \mathbf{b}_3. The fourth number is the origin shift (typically 0.5 to avoid high-symmetry points or 0.0 for Γ\Gamma-centered meshes).

Dimensionality Rules for K-Points

  • 3D Bulk Crystals (e.g. Silicon, Perovskite bulk): Full 3D grid (6×6×66 \times 6 \times 6 or 8×8×88 \times 8 \times 8).

  • 2D Surfaces & Slabs: Strictly 1 point along zz (kz=1k_z = 1, e.g. 4×4×14 \times 4 \times 1). Because the slab has vacuum along zz, there is zero physical dispersion along the reciprocal zz-axis. Setting kz>1k_z > 1 wastes massive compute power with zero physical benefit.

  • 1D Nanowires / Nanotubes: 1 point along xx and yy (1×1×81 \times 1 \times 8).

  • 0D Molecules / Isolated Clusters: Single Γ\Gamma-point calculation (1×1×11 \times 1 \times 1).

Structural Relaxation, General Constraints & Molecular Dynamics

Geometry Optimization Algorithms

Structural relaxation minimizes the total energy with respect to nuclear positions and cell vectors until atomic forces and stress tensors fall below strict tolerances:

MD.TypeOfRun       CG
MD.NumCGsteps      200
MD.MaxForceTol     0.04 eV/Ang
MD.MaxStressTol    1.0 GPa
MD.VariableCell    false

  • MD.TypeOfRun: Options include CG (Conjugate Gradient, standard), Broyden (quasi-Newton), and FIRE (Fast Inertial Relaxation Engine, superior for complex interfaces and loose initial configurations).

  • MD.MaxForceTol: The maximum force component allowed on any atom for convergence (typically 0.02–0.04 eV/Å0.02\text{--}0.04\text{ eV/\AA}).

  • MD.VariableCell: Set to true to relax cell vectors simultaneously. Critical Warning: Never enable MD.VariableCell true on a surface slab without fixing the cc-axis, or the simulation will compress the vacuum gap!

SIESTA Section 7.7: Use of General Constraints

SIESTA provides a versatile constraints engine (%block Geometry.Constraints) to freeze or couple degrees of freedom during relaxation:

%block Geometry.Constraints
  atom [1 -- 12]              # Freeze atoms 1 through 12 completely
  atom 15  0.  0.  1.         # Fix atom 15 along Z only (allow XY relaxation)
  cell-vector C               # Freeze out-of-plane vacuum axis
  stress XX YY                # Constrain in-plane stress components
  Z 82                        # Freeze all Lead (Pb) atoms by atomic number
%endblock Geometry.Constraints

Key Constraint Syntax Rules

  • Range specifications: Supports atom [1 – 4], atom from 1 to 4 step 1, and space-separated lists atom 1 2 3 4.

  • Directional Constraints: Append three real numbers representing a Cartesian projection vector. Crucial Rule: Reals must include a trailing decimal point (e.g., 0. 0. 1.). SIESTA subtracts the force component projected onto this vector: 𝐅active=𝐅−(𝐅⋅𝐧̂)𝐧̂\mathbf{F}_{\text{active}} = \mathbf{F} - (\mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}) \hat{\mathbf{n}}

  • Cell Constraints: cell-vector C locks the vacuum height, allowing in-plane cell vectors AA and BB to relax without collapsing the vacuum.

Work Function Determination (Φ=Vvac−EF\Phi = V_{\text{vac}} - E_F)

The work function Φ\Phi is the minimum energy required to remove an electron from the Fermi level of a solid to the electrostatic vacuum plateau outside the surface: Φ=Vvac−EF\Phi = V_{\text{vac}} - E_F

  1. Request the electrostatic potential grid: SaveElectrostaticPotential true ⟹\implies generates SystemLabel.VH.

  2. Compute planar average V‾(z)=1A∬V(𝐫)dxdy\bar{V}(z) = \frac{1}{A} \iint V(\mathbf{r}) dx dy using Util/Macroave or grid2cube.

  3. Identify the asymptotic flat plateau VvacV_{\text{vac}} in the middle of the vacuum region.

  4. Subtract the self-consistent Fermi level: Φ=Vvac−EF\Phi = V_{\text{vac}} - E_F.

The SIESTA Input Builder Web Studio Manual

Tutorial 3: Gas Adsorption on Surface — Energy & Charge Density Difference

Objective: Calculate the binding energy EadsE_{\text{ads}} of a water molecule (H2OH_2O) on a TiO2TiO_2 anatase surface, and compute the planar charge transfer Δρ(z)\Delta\rho(z).

The Three Independent Calculations

To compute the adsorption energy: Eads=Etotal[Slab+Mol]−Eslab[Clean Slab]−Emol[Isolated Mol]E_{\text{ads}} = E_{\text{total}}[\text{Slab}+\text{Mol}] - E_{\text{slab}}[\text{Clean Slab}] - E_{\text{mol}}[\text{Isolated Mol}]

  1. System 1 (Total): H2OH_2O adsorbed on TiO2TiO_2 slab in cell a×b×ca \times b \times c. Output: Etotal=−14256.789 eVE_{\text{total}} = -14256.789\text{ eV}.

  2. System 2 (Clean Slab): Bare TiO2TiO_2 slab in the exact same cell a×b×ca \times b \times c. Output: Eslab=−13850.123 eVE_{\text{slab}} = -13850.123\text{ eV}.

  3. System 3 (Free Molecule): Isolated H2OH_2O molecule in the exact same cell. Output: Emol=−405.892 eVE_{\text{mol}} = -405.892\text{ eV}.

Eads=−14256.789−(−13850.123)−(−405.892)=−0.774 eV(Exothermic Physisorption)E_{\text{ads}} = -14256.789 - (-13850.123) - (-405.892) = -0.774\text{ eV} \quad (\text{Exothermic Physisorption})

Planar Average Charge Density Difference (CDD)

Request valence charge densities: SaveRho true. Generate 3D grid files: Δρ(𝐫)=ρtotal(𝐫)−ρslab(𝐫)−ρmol(𝐫)\Delta\rho(\mathbf{r}) = \rho_{\text{total}}(\mathbf{r}) - \rho_{\text{slab}}(\mathbf{r}) - \rho_{\text{mol}}(\mathbf{r}) Integrate in the xyxy-plane to yield the planar profile: Δρ‾(z)=1A∬Δρ(x,y,z)dxdy\Delta\bar{\rho}(z) = \frac{1}{A} \iint \Delta\rho(x,y,z) dx dy and the cumulative charge transfer across the interface: ΔQ(z)=∫−∞zΔρ‾(z′)dz′\Delta Q(z) = \int_{-\infty}^z \Delta\bar{\rho}(z') dz'

In **1⃝2 Surface & Work Function Studio →\to Planar Average CDD**, uploading the resulting .cube or .dat file renders the dual-axis chart:

  • Peak accumulation region (+Δρ+\Delta\rho: electron buildup at the interface bond).

  • Peak depletion region (−Δρ-\Delta\rho: charge loss from the substrate surface).

  • Net transferred charge Δq\Delta q and interface dipole Δμ\Delta\mu in Debye.
